title: The right triangle

## What you saw

A right triangle has one 90° angle and two sharp ones. Pick one of the sharp angles and call it **θ** (“theta”). Now the sides get names from where they sit relative to θ:

- the **hypotenuse** is the longest side, opposite the right angle;
- the **opposite** side faces θ;
- the **adjacent** side touches θ (and isn’t the hypotenuse).

Trigonometry starts with three ratios:

| | ratio | |
|---|---|---|
| <span class="c-sin">sin θ</span> | opposite / hypotenuse | “how much it climbs” |
| <span class="c-cos">cos θ</span> | adjacent / hypotenuse | “how far it goes along” |
| <span class="c-tan">tan θ</span> | opposite / adjacent | the slope |

## The most important thing

When you made the triangle bigger without changing the angle, the ratios **stayed the same**. Two triangles with the same angles are *similar*: one is just a scaled-up copy of the other. That’s why sin 30° is always 0.5, however big the triangle. The ratios depend only on the angle, so they can be worked out once and put in a **table**. The lesson packet starts with exactly such a table, from 1° to 90°.

## Pythagoras in new clothes

Pythagoras’ theorem says opposite² + adjacent² = hypotenuse². Divide everything by hypotenuse²:

> (opposite/hypotenuse)² + (adjacent/hypotenuse)² = 1, that is **sin²θ + cos²θ = 1**

This is the first identity in the packet. Divide by adjacent² or by opposite² instead and you get two more: 1 + tan²θ = sec²θ and 1 + cot²θ = csc²θ.

## What it’s good for

If you know one angle and one side, you can find the others without measuring them. Surveyors measured the height of mountains this way: walk a known distance away, measure the angle up to the top, and **height = distance · tan(angle)**.

> For the curious: there are three more ratios, the upside-down versions of the first three: sec θ = 1/cos θ, csc θ = 1/sin θ, cot θ = 1/tan θ.
